# Why the Natufians lived in the oak–pistachio belt

*An independent, sourced note on the risks and rewards of woodland, parkland, closed forest and steppe for Levantine foragers, about 15,000–11,500 years ago, and what a simulation needs to reproduce the choice. Written October 2026.*

## Summary

The Natufian "homeland" (Bar-Yosef 1998; Bar-Yosef and Valla 1991) is the band of open Mediterranean woodland and parkland – deciduous and evergreen oak, terebinth/pistachio, almond, with a grassy understorey rich in wild cereals – running through the Galilee, Mount Carmel, the Judean hills and the Jordan Valley margins. The best reading of the evidence is not that this belt was the single richest country, but that it was the place where **several reliable, storable, seasonally staggered foods lay within a day's walk of permanent water**, with good visibility and easy movement. Closed forest offered less food at ground level and more danger for little gain; steppe offered gazelle, tubers and seasonal waterfowl but less reliable plant staples and scarcer water. Two caveats matter. First, the "core area" model is now contested: Shubayqa 1 in the north-east Jordanian steppe is as early as the woodland sites (Richter et al. 2017). Second, true closed forest was rare in the southern Levant at that time, so "why not dense forest?" is partly a question that the landscape itself did not often pose.

## 1. Climate and shifting vegetation belts

- **Late Glacial Maximum to the Bølling–Allerød.** A high-resolution pollen record from Jordan River Dureijat on palaeolake Hula (Langgut, Cheddadi and Sharon 2021, *Quaternary Science Reviews*) finds the warmest and wettest stretch of the whole Epipalaeolithic between about 14.9 and 13.0 ka cal BP, peaking near 14.5 ka with about 545 mm of rain a year. Heat-loving trees – deciduous oak, olive, *Pistacia* – increase. The authors explicitly tie this interval to the onset of the Natufian and of sedentism.
- **Hula and Ghab cores.** The older pollen diagrams (Baruch and Bottema 1991, 1999) also show arboreal pollen rising in the Bølling–Allerød and falling in the Younger Dryas, as do lake levels at Hula and Lake Lisan (the Pleistocene Dead Sea), which stood high in the Bølling–Allerød and dropped at the start of the Younger Dryas.
- **Younger Dryas (about 12.9–11.7 ka).** The classic view (Moore and Hillman 1992; Bar-Yosef 1998) is that it was cold and dry, the woodland retreated, wild cereal stands thinned and Late Natufian groups became more mobile. This is now debated. Langgut et al. (2021) find the Younger Dryas cold but with low seasonal contrast and more rain outside winter; gazelle-tooth isotopes from Hayonim and Hilazon Tachtit (Hartman et al. 2016, *PNAS*) indicate cooling but **not** drying. The practical point holds either way: the belt between forest and steppe moved over decades to centuries, and its edges were the places where shifts hit first.

**Uncertain:** how much of the southern Levant carried genuinely closed canopy at any moment. Most reconstructions describe open woodland, maquis and parkland, with denser forest confined to the wetter north (Lebanon, coastal Syria, higher Galilee).

## 2. Food in each kind of country

### Oak–pistachio parkland and woodland

- **Acorns** are the largest potential calorie crop of Mediterranean woodland, ripen in autumn, and store for months once dried. Direct Natufian acorn remains are scarce, partly because acorns preserve badly and are processed into meal. The case rests on the many bedrock mortars, deep stone mortars and pestles at sites such as Hayonim, el-Wad and Raqefet, and on charcoal showing oak dominating the local woodland (el-Wad Terrace: Caracuta et al. 2016; Mount Carmel generally). Use-wear on Natufian grinding tools (Dubreuil 2004) shows varied plant processing, with legumes and cereals becoming more common late in the period. **Debated:** whether acorns were a staple or one food among many. Ethnographic California is the strongest analogy – oak-woodland peoples with stored acorns reached among the highest forager densities recorded (Baumhoff 1963; Binford 2001) – but it is an analogy, not evidence.
- **Pistachio (terebinth, *Pistacia atlantica/palaestina*) and wild almond** give oily nuts, also storable. Their charcoal and nutshell are common in Epipalaeolithic and early Neolithic sites.
- **Wild cereals and legumes.** Wild barley, emmer and einkorn grow thickly in the grassy openings of the parkland and on its steppe side. Harlan's (1967) experiment harvesting wild einkorn in south-east Turkey gathered roughly 2 kg of spikelets (about 1 kg of clean grain) an hour with a flint sickle, enough for a family to gather a year's supply in about three weeks. That is the main reason cereal stands are thought to have repaid a settled life. At Ohalo II, on the Sea of Galilee long before the Natufian (about 23 ka), more than 140 plant taxa were collected, including wild barley and wheat and many small-seeded grasses (Weiss et al. 2004). Phytoliths from Raqefet (Power et al. 2014) show both small- and large-grained grasses. Arranz-Otaegui et al. (2018) caution that the Natufian plant diet was broader than the "founder crops", with many small seeds, sedges and tubers.
- **Gazelle.** Mountain gazelle (*Gazella gazella*) dominates almost every woodland Natufian bone assemblage, often well over half of the ungulates, with fallow deer, roe deer, wild boar, aurochs and wild goat in smaller numbers, plus hares, tortoises, birds and fish (Davis 1983; Bar-Oz 2004; Munro 2004). Gazelle is a creature of open country and edges: it grazes in grassland and parkland and is easiest to intercept where hills meet plains.

### Closed forest

Little direct Levantine evidence exists, so the argument is ecological. A closed canopy puts most of its production out of reach (in leaves and wood high up), shades out grasses and herbs, and gives fewer, more scattered nut-bearing trees per hectare than an open parkland where trees get full light and crop heavily. Ungulates of closed forest (red and roe deer, wild boar) live at lower densities and are harder to locate than open-country herds. This is the general reason given for low forager densities in closed forests worldwide (Kelly 2013; Binford 2001).

### Steppe

The Irano-Turanian steppe east and south of the woodland had gazelle in large numbers, onager, hare, and in wetlands seasonal waterfowl. Shubayqa 1 (Richter et al. 2017; Yeomans, Richter and Martin 2017) shows Early Natufians there hunting gazelle and many birds and relying heavily on club-rush (*Bolboschoenus*) tubers and other wild plants (Arranz-Otaegui et al. 2018). Steppe can feed a band well, but its riches are more patchy, more tied to a few wet places, and more vulnerable to a dry year.

### Predictability and storage

The woodland belt's real advantage is a **staggered calendar of storable foods**: cereals and legumes ripen in spring–early summer, almonds in summer, pistachio and acorns in autumn, with gazelle and smaller game available all year. Each crop fails in some years, but rarely all together. Storage then bridges winter and the lean late summer. Steppe offers fewer storable staples and swings harder with rainfall; closed forest offers fewer staples at ground level. Rosen and Rivera-Collazo (2012, *PNAS*) argue that Early Natufian investment in "narrowly focused, high-yield plant resources" was possible because of the warm, wet Bølling–Allerød, and that Late Natufians broadened their diet and moved more when conditions worsened.

## 3. The edge: several zones within a day's walk

The classic sites sit where different countries meet:

- **Mount Carmel caves** (el-Wad, Kebara, Raqefet, Nahal Oren): oak–terebinth woodland on the hill, the coastal plain with its marshes and gazelle pastures below, the sea a few kilometres away.
- **Ain Mallaha (Eynan)**: on the slope above the Hula lake, by a spring, with hills behind and lake and marsh in front. Tens of thousands of fish bones, including trout, come from its final layers (Borvon et al. 2018).
- **Hayonim Cave and Terrace**: western Galilee, woodland valley with access to the coastal plain.
- **Wadi Hammeh 27**: near Pella in the Jordan Valley, by springs, between the valley floor and the woodland of the Transjordanian hills (Edwards 2013), dated to about 14.5–14.0 ka.
- **Shubayqa 1**: on the edge of a steppe wetland, between basalt desert and pools.

This is the "hilly flanks" idea first put forward by Braidwood (1960) for the origins of farming: the wild ancestors of crops and herd animals were native to the uplands and foothills of the Fertile Crescent, so the people who used them lived there. Later work (Henry 1989; Bar-Yosef 1998) refined it into an **ecotone model**: settled base camps on the boundary between Mediterranean woodland and steppe, at middle elevations, where a forager walking 10 km in a day can reach upland nuts, valley cereals, plain or steppe gazelle and wetland fish and birds. An edge evens out bad years: if the acorns fail, the cereals or the marsh may not.

**Debated:** whether the edge is a cause or a consequence. Shubayqa shows an Early Natufian thriving outside the woodland, so the woodland is not a precondition (Richter et al. 2017). But Shubayqa is itself an edge – between steppe and wetland – which supports the general principle more than it refutes it.

## 4. Dangers and costs of denser forest

Here the direct Levantine evidence is thinnest, and much rests on ecology and ethnography.

- **Predators.** The Late Pleistocene Levant had leopard, lion, wolf, brown bear, striped hyena and smaller cats. Their bones occur, rarely, in Epipalaeolithic and Natufian faunal lists. Ambush predators, above all leopard and lion, hunt from cover; across modern Africa and Asia, attacks on people concentrate where dense vegetation lets a predator approach unseen (Treves and Naughton-Treves 1999). Among the Agta of the Philippine forest, Headland and Greene (2011) recorded that about a quarter of adult men had survived a python attack. In open parkland people see danger at a distance and can defend in groups; in closed forest they cannot. **Uncertain:** no Natufian skeleton is known to me with clear marks of a predator kill, and the scale of the risk is inferred.
- **Visibility and movement.** Closed forest slows walking, hides game, makes tracking harder and makes it easier to get lost or be surprised by other people. Open woodland gives shade and fuel while keeping sight lines.
- **Disease and parasites.** Damp, shaded ground favours ticks, leeches, gut parasites and fungal infection; wetland margins in the warm season favour mosquitoes. Pathogen diversity rises with warmth and wetness (Guernier, Hochberg and Guégan 2004). The Hula marshes were badly malarious in historical times, until drained in the 1950s; whether malaria existed there in the Pleistocene is **unknown**. Note that Mediterranean forest is not tropical rainforest, so this cost is milder than in the tropics.
- **Game density.** Forest ungulates are solitary or in small groups and live at lower biomass per square kilometre than open-country grazers. A Natufian band that depended on gazelle would find few in closed forest.
- **Plant food at ground level.** Under a closed canopy there are few grasses or herbs and fewer, less heavily fruiting nut trees than in open woodland.

### Absence of sites in dense woodland

The Natufian record is concentrated in open woodland and its edges; there are few sites in the humid, steep, more densely forested mountains of Lebanon or the Ansariyah range, though survey there has also been weaker, so the absence is **partly a matter of where archaeologists have looked**. Dederiyeh Cave in north-west Syria, with charcoal of oak, elm, pistachio, maple, almond and ash, shows Natufians also used richer, more mixed woodland in the north – but still a valley location with access to open country.

## 5. Water

Every major sedentary Natufian site is at a permanent spring or lake: Ain Mallaha at the Hula, Wadi Hammeh 27 at springs feeding the Jordan Valley, the Carmel caves near springs and wadis, Shubayqa at a steppe pool system. Lake Lisan and the Hula stood high in the Bølling–Allerød. In the Mediterranean climate, water is plentiful in winter but scarce from June to October; a base camp must be near water that lasts the dry season. On the steppe, water is the limiting factor: Shubayqa's occupation is tied to the wetland, and Yeomans and Richter's work on nearby Shubayqa 6 shows the seasonal pull of winter waterfowl. Water also concentrates game, which makes the spring-side edge a hunting place as well as a camp.

## 6. Ethnography: forager densities and safety by kind of country

- **Closed tropical forest.** Bailey et al. (1989) and Headland (1987) argued that no foragers live in tropical rainforest independent of farmers: the Mbuti, Efe, Baka and Agta trade forest meat and honey for cultivated starch. This "wild yam question" is contested (Bahuchet, McKey and de Garine 1991; Sri Lankan archaeology back to about 38 ka; Baka expeditions living on wild yams for months), but even critics agree that densities are low and that carbohydrates are the bottleneck.
- **Global patterns.** In Binford's (2001) and Kelly's (2013) compilations, forager densities are highest where storable plant foods or aquatic resources are abundant – oak-woodland California, the salmon coasts – and low both in closed forest and in arid steppe. Mobility is highest where food is patchy and unpredictable.
- **Safety.** Predation and snakebite are real causes of forager deaths, typically a few per cent of deaths (Hill and Hurtado 1996 for the Aché of forested Paraguay, where jaguar and snakebite both appear; Headland and Greene 2011). Fire, dogs, group size and vigilance are the usual defences; Ain Mallaha's puppy burial (Davis and Valla 1978) shows Natufians had dogs.

## Conclusions

1. The oak–pistachio parkland combined the most **storable and staggered** foods (acorns, nuts, cereals, legumes) with the most important game (gazelle) and with permanent springs and lakes. That is what made year-round base camps worth building.
2. The **edge** mattered more than the belt itself: base camps sat where woodland met valley, plain, lake or steppe, so that one failed resource could be replaced by another within a day's walk.
3. **Closed forest** offered less food at ground level, sparser game, poorer visibility and higher risk of ambush and parasites, for little gain; it was in any case rare in the southern Levant.
4. **Steppe** could support Natufians, as Shubayqa shows, but only at wetlands and with more mobility and more risk in dry years.
5. As the climate shifted, the belt moved and the Natufians' range moved with it; the Younger Dryas pushed towards broader diets and more mobility, though how dry it was is debated.

## What this means for the model

For bands to prefer woodland and parkland edges for real reasons, rather than because a rule says so, the simulation needs:

1. **Staggered, storable plant crops by kind of country.** Acorn and pistachio mast in autumn in oak woodland and parkland; wild cereals and legumes in spring–early summer in parkland openings and steppe edge; tubers at steppe wetlands. Each with its own year-to-year variation, weakly correlated between crops, and with storage losses over months.
2. **Canopy shading of ground plant food.** Ground-level plant yield (grasses, herbs, cereals) falls as tree cover rises; nut yield per tree rises with light, so open woodland outyields closed forest.
3. **Gazelle and other grazers living at higher density in open and edge country**, lower in closed forest; forest ungulates solitary and scattered.
4. **Hunting success depending on visibility**: harder to find and approach game in thick cover; easier at edges and water where game gathers.
5. **Foraging radius over several cells**: a band's yield should sum what it can reach in a day, so that mixed neighbourhoods score higher than uniform ones – this is what makes an edge pay without an "edge bonus".
6. **Dry-season water as a hard need**, with springs and lakes as fixed points, seasonal pools that dry up, and lake levels following climate.
7. **Predation risk rising with cover** (already partly in place): ambush predators more dangerous in thick cover, lowered by fire, dogs, group size and vigilance.
8. **Movement cost rising with cover and slope**, reducing how far gatherers and hunters can range in a day.
9. **Disease load rising with damp, warmth and crowding** (partly in place): marsh fever by warm marshes, flux in fouled crowded camps; mild forest parasite load in damp closed woodland.
10. **Climate shifting the vegetation belts** over decades: woodland spreading in warm, wet phases and retreating in cold or dry ones, so that edge locations move and bands have to follow or broaden their diet.
11. **Steppe viability limited by water and rainfall variance**, not by a ban: steppe bands should succeed at wetlands in good years and suffer in droughts.

None of these needs a preference for a biome to be written in; the preference should emerge from food, water, danger and movement.
