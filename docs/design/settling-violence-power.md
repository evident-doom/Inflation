# Settling, violence and power: what the record says, and what it means for the model

Notes, 2026-10-14. These are from memory of the literature (authors named for following up). The figures are approximate.

## Violence: mobile and settled

**Mobile foragers are not peaceful, but their violence is mostly personal.**
- Ethnography (Fry and Söderberg; Kelly) finds that killings among mobile foragers are mostly one-on-one: revenge, rivalry over partners, executions of troublemakers. Group raids are rarer. Quarrels end by moving away.
- Estimates of violent death vary widely, from a few percent to over 15% (Bowles' compilation), depending on the people and the method.

**Early mass violence among foragers:**
- Jebel Sahaba (Nile, about 13,000–18,000 years ago): many skeletons with projectile wounds, often cited as the earliest warfare.
- Nataruk (Kenya, about 10,000 years ago): foragers massacred.

Both are on rich, fixed resources (a river, a lake shore): contested places.

**Natufian (Bocquentin and Bar-Yosef):** few injuries on skeletons, little sign of warfare. Early settling in the Levant was not especially violent.

**Neolithic:**
- Jericho's wall and tower (about 11,000 years ago) are a debated case: defence, flood control or ritual.
- Çatalhöyük had no walls.
- Central European Linear Pottery sites (about 7,000 years ago: Talheim, Schöneck-Kilianstädten, Herxheim) show whole communities killed.
- Ditches and palisades spread with farming in many regions.

**Contact between the mobile and the settled:**
- Both trade and raid: exchange, intermarriage, and raiding on stores.
- Later pastoralists and farmers show a long cycle: nomads raid or rule the sown, then settle and soften (Ibn Khaldun).
- On the European forager–farmer frontier there was both coexistence and violence, and the farmers spread mostly by their numbers.
- The settled are the target, because they have stores and cannot leave. The mobile can hit and vanish. Settlers answer with walls, alliances and paid protection.

## How settling changes people and their customs

- **Health:**
  - more infectious disease (crowding, refuse, commensal pests, water);
  - tooth decay from starchy food;
  - often shorter stature among early farmers.
- **Fertility and numbers:** shorter birth intervals (sedentism, weaning foods), the "Neolithic demographic transition" (Bocquet-Appel). Numbers rise fast despite more disease.
- **Property and storage:** household stores and ownership, inheritance, less demand sharing. Walls make stores private.
- **Living together:** bigger, permanent communities need new ways to settle disputes without moving away:
  - leaders and councils;
  - ritual intensification (Göbekli Tepe's enclosures, plastered skulls, communal buildings);
  - ancestor cults binding people to place (Natufian burial under floors).
- **Work:** more drudgery (grinding, tending), more division of labour by sex and craft.
- **World view:** the house and the domesticated world at the centre (Hodder's "domus"). Wild and tame, inside and outside, become meaningful.

## Power: when and why it arises

- **Mobile foragers are actively egalitarian.** They level aggrandisers with mockery, sharing demands and leaving (Boehm's "reverse dominance hierarchy"). Hunters play down their kills.
- **The preconditions for lasting inequality:**
  - storable surplus;
  - sedentism;
  - defensible, rich places (Dyson-Hudson and Smith);
  - wealth that can be inherited (Borgerhoff Mulder, Mattison: material wealth passes down and compounds in farming and herding, while forager wealth is mostly skills and ties);
  - population pressure in circumscribed land (Carneiro).
- **Aggrandisers and feasting (Hayden):** ambitious individuals use surplus to host feasts that create debts and prestige. The potlatch of the Pacific Northwest is a forager case: rank, slaves and hereditary titles without farming.
- **The stages**, roughly: egalitarian bands; transegalitarian communities with achieved status (big men, feast-givers); chiefdoms with inherited rank and redistribution. Natufian rich burials (shell headdresses, the shaman of Hilazon Tachtit) hint at standing; marked inheritance appears in the later Neolithic.

## What it means for the model

The model already has stores, houses, feasts with ties, gifts, ornaments, grave goods, raids, palisades, claims and customs.

**Missing or thin:**
1. **Violence within bands** (personal quarrels, revenge, executions). It is the most common forager violence and is rising with crowding and settling. Today a band's inner conflict shows only as earlier splits.
2. **Raids that pay**, with the target's stores and the raiders' mobility mattering (partly built). Nomad–settled frontiers will emerge once herders exist.
3. **Fertility rising with settling** (already built) together with disease (built). Check the balance as houses spread.
4. **Levelling customs against aggrandisers.**
   - A band's **standing** is already shaped by ornaments.
   - Add **individual prestige inside a band**, held by a leader: it grows by hosting feasts, giving gifts and leading successful raids, and counts in decisions (raids, feasts, appointments).
   - A **levelling custom** caps it.
   - **Inherited wealth** (stores, houses, herds, ornaments passing down) lets prestige pass to heirs and become rank.

   Rank then appears where surplus, storage, defensible places and inheritance meet, and not elsewhere.
5. **Ritual centres:** gathering places with built monuments (labour pooled across bands), binding ties and claims at the scale of a region.
